## The Room Gets Smaller Every Month

At first, it is just one missed birthday party.

Then it is the WhatsApp group she used to reply to within minutes, now left on read for three days because "revision hai."

Then it is the weekend trip her school friends went on without her, the group photo she looks at twice and closes.

By the time NEET or JEE results are out, some students realise something odd. They got the rank. They did not get a single new memory outside a textbook in eighteen months. The room they have been living in — mentally and sometimes literally — has been shrinking the entire time, and nobody warned them it would happen this quietly.

This is not a story about laziness or lack of discipline. It is a story about what happens when an entire academic culture teaches young people that a social life and a serious future are mutually exclusive. And the honest answer, backed by both research and the daily reality of Indian classrooms and coaching hubs, is that this belief is not just wrong. It is dangerous.

## The Numbers Nobody Puts on a Report Card

Ask any coaching institute in Kota, Delhi or Hyderabad how many hours a serious NEET or JEE aspirant studies daily, and the number will sound almost normal to Indian ears — twelve, fourteen hours is not unusual. What rarely gets discussed with the same casualness is what that schedule does to a young mind.

Several independent studies over the past few years have pointed to the same pattern: a large share of Indian college and exam-prep students report anxiety levels that would be considered clinically significant, with competitive exam aspirants consistently reporting some of the highest stress scores among any student group in the country. Isolation, fractured sleep and constant comparison with peers online show up again and again as contributing factors — not just the difficulty of the syllabus itself.

The pattern is consistent enough that the Supreme Court of India issued binding directions in 2025 requiring every educational institution with over a hundred students to appoint a qualified counsellor and display crisis helpline numbers. That such a mandate was needed at the highest judicial level tells you how deep the problem runs, and how invisible it has been allowed to remain inside individual households and classrooms.

None of this is meant to alarm. It is meant to make one thing obvious: the isolation that exam culture normalises is not a side effect. For a meaningful number of students, it is the actual mechanism of harm.

## Meet Ananya: Viman Nagar, Pune

Ananya moved from Nashik to Pune at seventeen to join a NEET coaching institute near Viman Nagar. Her day began at 5:30 AM and ended past midnight, cycling between two coaching batches, a hostel room she shared with a stranger, and a phone she mostly used for mock test notifications.

For the first year, she told herself this was temporary — a necessary sacrifice for two years, after which "real life" would resume. What she had not accounted for was how hard it would be to rebuild a social muscle that had gone unused for that long. When her second attempt finally succeeded and she got into a medical college in Pune itself, she found herself sitting in a hostel common room full of new classmates, unable to remember the last time she had made a new friend without a syllabus attached to the conversation.

It took her a full semester of deliberately saying yes to things — a college trek, a friend's cousin's birthday, eventually a Stranger Mingle board game evening near Koregaon Park — before she noticed the change. She was laughing at things that were not funny in an exam-relevant way. She was, for the first time in years, simply spending time with people because it felt good, not because it was productive.

Ananya's story is not unusual. It is close to the median experience of a serious Indian exam aspirant. What is unusual is that she eventually did something about it.

## Isolation vs Independence: Knowing the Difference

Not all solitude during exam preparation is harmful. Focused, temporary withdrawal to hit a deadline is a normal and often necessary part of serious academic work. The difficulty is that many students, families and even coaching institutes fail to distinguish between healthy focus and slow, corrosive isolation.

| Signal | Healthy Focus | Harmful Isolation |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Weeks before a specific exam | Months or years, indefinitely |
| Social contact | Reduced but intentional, planned around study blocks | Near zero, replaced entirely by study or scrolling |
| Emotional state | Tired but purposeful | Numb, flat, or persistently anxious |
| Identity | "I am a person who is currently studying hard" | "I am only my rank, my score, my result" |
| Recovery | Bounces back socially once the exam passes | Struggles to reconnect even after the exam is over |
| Support system | Friends and family check in and are allowed to | Contact is actively avoided, even from people who care |

If your preparation, or your child's, or your friend's, consistently falls in the right column rather than the left, that is not commitment. That is a warning sign worth taking seriously.

## Why "Focus Karo, Baaki Sab Baad Mein" Is Incomplete Advice

Every Indian student preparing for a competitive exam has heard some version of this line from a parent, relative or well-meaning teacher: focus now, everything else can wait.

The intention behind it is not malicious. Families genuinely believe that eliminating distraction improves outcomes. But the advice treats social connection as a distraction rather than what it actually is for most young people — a regulatory system. Friendships and community are not the opposite of academic performance. They are frequently what makes sustained academic performance possible in the first place.

Research on student stress consistently shows that peer support and a sense of belonging act as a buffer against burnout and are strongly associated with better emotional regulation during high-pressure periods. Students who maintain even a small, low-effort social circle during intense preparation tend to report better sleep, better emotional stability, and paradoxically, better ability to sit down and actually study when it matters — because a mind that gets occasional relief does not run on empty for eighteen months straight.

Cutting out everything except the syllabus does not create more mental bandwidth. Past a certain point, it quietly drains the very bandwidth it is trying to protect.

## What Rebuilding a Social Circle Actually Looks Like

For students deep into exam preparation, or young professionals who spent their most formative years in this exact pattern and now find themselves at twenty-four with no social muscle left, rebuilding does not require grand gestures. It requires small, repeatable choices.

- Start with low-stakes, structured social settings rather than open-ended plans that feel overwhelming to organise. A board game evening or a group trek has a built-in activity, which removes the pressure of "what do we even talk about."
- Choose environments where nobody already knows your rank, your college or your family's expectations. New, low-context social spaces let you exist as a full person rather than as a walking result.
- Treat social time as scheduled, not optional. The same discipline that built a 14-hour study routine can build a two-hour weekly social routine. It does not need to compete with study time; it needs one dedicated slot.
- Prioritise verified, safe spaces, especially for young women navigating a new city alone for the first time. Anonymous or unverified group hangouts add a layer of risk that defeats the purpose of building genuine connection.

## The Real Cost of Waiting Until "After"

The most common reason students give for postponing their social life is a version of the same sentence: I will make friends after this exam, after this result, after this phase.

The problem is that "after" rarely arrives cleanly. One exam gives way to the next milestone — a college, then a placement, then a promotion. If the habit of connecting with people is never built, there is no natural point at which it starts happening on its own. The skill of showing up, making small talk with strangers, and building a friendship from scratch atrophies exactly like any other skill that goes unused for years.

Ananya could have waited for "after MBBS" to start building friendships. Plenty of her batchmates did exactly that, and many are still waiting, telling themselves the same story two, three, five years later. The students who break the pattern are the ones who treat a two-hour social evening with the same seriousness as a mock test — not more important, but not disposable either.
